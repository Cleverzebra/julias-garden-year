Your zone

Type a US ZIP code and the 2023 USDA map answers. Or pick a zone by hand.

Zone is the coldest winter your yard usually sees, in 5-degree bands. It decides what survives, not what thrives; sun, soil, and water do the rest. Frost dates shown are typical, not guaranteed; a valley or a hilltop can run two weeks either way.

What this is, and what it is not

Groundwork is a reference for temperate gardens, written with the northeastern United States in front of it (zones 4 to 8) and general rules stated where they hold everywhere. It covers trees, shrubs, perennials, grasses, vines, bulbs, annuals, and the common edibles. It does not cover lawns, houseplants, or the tropics.

Care and timing advice was drafted from university extension services and botanical gardens, listed on the sources page, and then edited into plain language. It is a starting point, and a good one, but your county extension office knows your soil and your pests better than any website. Where a fact could not be confirmed against a source it is marked as such rather than stated with false confidence.

Plant identifications from photographs are always guesses until someone standing in the garden confirms them. The photo kit is built around that rule.

Plant library

About 230 plants, with the parts people get wrong

Each card carries the facts that decide whether a plant lives (zone, sun, water), the facts that decide whether you like it in five years (mature size, deer, pests), and the one warning that matters. Search by common name, Latin name, or any word in the notes: try salt, wet, toxic, or hummingbird.

Deer ratings follow the Rutgers scale (A rarely damaged, B seldom severely, C occasionally severely, D frequently severely) where Rutgers lists the plant; otherwise they are inferred from extension notes and marked as such in the card. Hungry deer in a hard winter eat plants they usually skip.

Pruning

When to cut what, and where the cut goes

Two rules cover most of the yard. Anything that flowers before the end of June carries its buds through winter on last year's wood, so prune it in the few weeks after it blooms. Anything that flowers after June blooms on wood it grows the same season, so prune it in late winter before growth starts. Dead, broken, and diseased wood can come off any day of the year. Nothing gets pruned after mid-August in the Northeast; the regrowth will not harden before frost.

Each group, in detail

The cuts themselves

Thinning cuts and heading cuts

A thinning cut removes a branch where it starts, at the trunk collar or at a side branch at least a third its size. It opens the plant, keeps the natural shape, and does not trigger a burst of regrowth. A heading cut shortens a branch to a bud or to nowhere in particular; it triggers a cluster of shoots below the cut. Thinning is the default for trees and most shrubs. Heading is for hedges, for pinching, and for rejuvenating shrubs that are meant to resprout.

How much in a year

About a quarter of a tree's live canopy at the outside, and less on an old tree; a third of a healthy shrub. Heavy corrective work gets spread over two or three years. One big limb off a mature tree can leave a wound that never closes.

Renewing an overgrown shrub

Two ways. The gentle one: cut a third of the oldest, thickest stems to the ground each year for three years, taking the others as new ones come up. The drastic one: cut the whole shrub to 6 to 12 inches in late winter; forsythia, lilac, privet, spirea, red-twig dogwood, ninebark, summersweet, and many others come back from that, and flower again in two years. Broadleaf evergreens mostly tolerate it (rhododendron, yew, holly, boxwood); needled evergreens except yew do not.

Never top a tree

Cutting the main limbs back to stubs starves the tree, opens every stub to decay, and produces a broom of weak shoots that break in the next storm. A topped tree is a removal on a delay. If a tree is too big for its spot, a crown reduction by an arborist cuts back to well-placed side limbs; if that will not do it, the tree is in the wrong place.

Wound dressings

Do not paint cuts. Dressings trap moisture, slow the woundwood, and stop nothing. The one exception is an oak or elm wounded between April and July, when the beetles that carry oak wilt and Dutch elm disease are flying; paint that cut within minutes with latex paint or a wound dressing.

Clean tools

Between plants, always; between every cut when removing diseased wood. Seventy percent isopropyl alcohol wiped on the blade works for most jobs. For fire blight, cankers, and black knot use one part household bleach to nine parts water, then rinse and oil the blade after, since bleach pits steel. Prune healthy plants first and the sick ones last.

Fire blight strikes

On apple, pear, crabapple, hawthorn, quince, and serviceberry: shoots that blacken from the tip and hook over like a shepherd's crook. In the growing season, cut 12 inches below the visible damage into clean wood, in dry weather, sanitizing between every cut, and burn or bag the prunings. Cankers seen in the dormant season are cut 6 to 12 inches below their lower edge. Never prune these trees during bloom or in warm humid weather when infection pressure is highest.

When to hire an arborist

Anything that needs a chainsaw off the ground, a ladder against the tree, or a limb you cannot control the fall of. Anything within 10 feet of a power line is not a job for you or for a general landscaper; branches touching lines belong to the utility, which will send a crew if you call. Hand tools from the ground, limbs under an inch with loppers and up to an inch and a half with a hand saw, are the homeowner's range.

Look for an ISA Certified Arborist, which means a passed exam and continuing education, and ask for proof of liability insurance. Walk away from anyone who knocks on the door saying your trees need work today, and from anyone who proposes topping. The ISA's free directory is at treesaregood.org/findanarborist.

The cheapest pruning is the pruning of a young tree. Ten minutes a year for the first five years, choosing one leader and spacing the main limbs along the trunk, prevents the split crotch and the crossing limb that cost hundreds of dollars to fix at year twenty. Remove nothing else from a young tree than what is dead, broken, or competing with the leader; the low branches feed the trunk and come off gradually later.

Plant doctor

Start from what you see, not from the name

Most sick plants are not diseased. They are too deep, too wet, too dry, in the wrong light, or recovering from something that happened two summers ago. This page walks from the symptom to the likely causes in order of probability, tells you what to check before you treat, and says plainly when a thing is cosmetic and can be ignored.

    When you need a lab, and where the labs are

    A diagnostic lab answers in a week for about the price of a shrub, and it is the only way to confirm verticillium, bacterial leaf scorch, root rot pathogens, and most tree cankers. Send fresh material (a branch with the transition from live to dead, roots with soil for wilts, several leaves at different stages), wrapped in dry paper towel in a bag, with photos of the whole plant. Call first for anything unusual.

    Choosing an arborist. Look for ISA certification and proof of liability and workers' compensation insurance, get a written estimate, and never pay in advance. Anyone who knocks on the door because "our crews are working in the neighborhood" is a red flag, and so is anyone who proposes topping. Have a tree examined when you see dead or falling branches, cavities, fungal shelves, or a new lean, and after any big storm. Directory: treesaregood.org/findanarborist.

    The year

    What the garden wants this month

    Written for zone 6, where the last frost lands in late April or early May and the first in mid-October. If you set your zone on the Start page, the frost dates below adjust and the note tells you how far to slide the timing.

    The list

      The two dates that organize everything

      The last spring frost decides when the tender things go out: tomatoes, basil, annuals, dahlias, and the sunflower and corn seed. Plant a week after the typical date, or the day after a ten-day forecast shows nothing under 40 degrees at night. Cool-season plants (pansies, peas, lettuce, spinach, chard, kale, most perennials, every tree and shrub) can go in six weeks before it.

      The first fall frost decides the other end: when to bring in the houseplants and ferns (before it, at 50-degree nights), when to lift dahlias (after it blackens them), when annuals are done, and when to plant garlic and bulbs (around it). Trees and shrubs planted in September and October root all fall and usually outgrow the same plant put in the following May.

      Typical is not guaranteed. The dates on this page are the middle of a range that varies by two weeks or more between a hilltop and the valley below it, and by year. A low spot where cold air pools frosts earlier and later than the map says. Your county extension office and the National Weather Service both publish local frost probabilities by date; the ZIP lookup at almanac.com/gardening/frostdates is the quickest check.

      Your yard, from photos

      How to get a whole garden analyzed from pictures

      A set of photographs and a few facts are enough for a careful analyst (human or machine) to name most of what is growing, map the beds, write a care card for each plant, and lay out the next twelve months. The quality of the answer depends almost entirely on the quality of the ask. This page is the ask.

      What to photograph

      • The whole yard from each side, standing at the property line and shooting toward the house. Four to six wide shots.
      • Each bed straight on, from about 10 feet, so the whole bed fits in the frame with the house or fence for scale.
      • Every plant you cannot name: one photo of the whole plant, one close-up of a leaf (top and underside), and one of a flower, fruit, or bud if there is any.
      • Trunks and bark of every tree and big shrub, at eye level, plus the base where it meets the soil.
      • Anything that looks wrong: the sick leaf next to a healthy one, the dead branch, the hole, the spot, the mushroom.
      • Plant tags, receipts, or the old planting plan, if any survive.
      • The same bed at 9 in the morning and 3 in the afternoon. Shadows tell the analyst which beds get sun.
      • The downspouts, the low spot that puddles, and the strip by the road that gets plowed salt.

      What a good analysis contains

      Compare whatever you get back against this. If it is missing a piece, ask for it.

      1. An inventory of every plant seen, each with a confidence label: certain, probable, or guess, and what would settle the guess.
      2. A map of the beds with the plants placed in them, however rough.
      3. A care card per plant: light, water, when it blooms, whether to deadhead, when and how to cut back, what commonly goes wrong.
      4. A month-by-month plan for the next year built from those specific plants.
      5. A list of what looks sick or badly sited, with the likely cause and the first thing to check.
      6. Hazards flagged: anything toxic to the animals or children you mentioned, anything invasive, anything about to hit the house or the wires.
      7. A short list of what it could not tell from the photos and what photo would answer it.
      Rules to hold the analyst to. Identifications from photographs are guesses until confirmed on the ground. No pruning advice on a shrub nobody has named; a wrong cut on a hydrangea costs a year of flowers. No confident instruction to rip out a "weed." Toxicity flags for pets and kids are mandatory, not optional. Regional timing must be tied to your zone, not to the analyst's hometown.

      The request, ready to paste

      Copy this into whatever assistant you use, attach the photographs, fill in the bracketed facts, and send it. It asks for exactly the pieces above, in that order, with the confidence labels and the safety rules built in. If a person built this site for you, send them the photos and the facts instead; this is the brief they will work from.

      Reading your own photos

      Before sending anything, three things you can read yourself. Shadow length and direction at 9 and 3 tell you which beds are really in sun; most "full sun" beds against a house get five hours. A dark, damp band at the bottom of a wall or a fence is the wet spot, and the plants there should be chosen for it. And a mulch cone piled up a trunk, or a trunk that goes straight into the ground like a telephone pole with no flare, is the single most common cause of a slowly dying tree; it is on the plant doctor page under abiotic causes.

      Basics

      The ten things that decide whether a plant lives

      Most plants that die in their first two years die of one of these, and none of them is a disease. They are planting depth, water, mulch, and site. Fix these and the pruning and pest pages become a lot less urgent.

      1. Find the root flare before you dig. Pull potting mix off the top of the root ball until you see where the trunk widens into roots. That flare sits at or slightly above the finished soil level. Nursery pots and burlap balls are routinely 2 to 4 inches too deep, and a buried flare rots bark and starves the tree over five to ten years.
      2. Dig wide, not deep. Two to three times the width of the root ball, no deeper than the ball itself, so the plant sits on undisturbed soil and does not sink. Rough up the sides of a slick clay hole.
      3. Cut circling roots. Container roots that wrap the pot keep wrapping in the ground and strangle the trunk a decade later. Slice through them vertically in three or four places, or shave the outer inch off the ball. Remove all wire, twine, and burlap from the top third of a balled tree.
      4. Backfill with the soil that came out. Not compost, not bagged garden soil. Amended holes become bathtubs in clay and cause roots to stay inside the soft pocket. Save the compost for the surface.
      5. Water it in, then keep watering for a year. A new tree wants about 10 gallons per inch of trunk diameter each week in the growing season, slowly, at the root ball, when rain does not supply it. Perennials and shrubs want a deep soak (an inch of water) once a week rather than a sprinkle every day. Year two, taper; year three, it is on its own except in drought.
      6. Mulch flat, 2 to 3 inches, and pull it back from the trunk. A mulch volcano piled against bark holds water against it, invites rot and rodents, and makes roots grow up into the mulch where they dry out. The mulch ring should look like a doughnut, not a cone.
      7. Do not fertilize a new plant. Roots come first. Fertilizer pushes leaf growth the roots cannot support and can burn fresh roots. A soil test from your state extension lab (about $20) tells you if anything is missing; most established landscapes need nothing.
      8. Stake only if it will not stand. A trunk that moves in the wind builds taper and strength. If you must stake, use two stakes, a loose flexible tie, and remove them after one growing season. Ties left on girdle trunks.
      9. Plant for the size on the tag, not the size in the pot. An arborvitae planted 2 feet from the siding is a removal in ten years. Read the mature width, halve it, and that is the distance from the wall, the walk, and the next plant.
      10. Match the plant to the light and water you actually have. A shade plant in afternoon sun scorches; a sun plant in shade stretches and never flowers; a dry-land plant in the wet corner rots. The library filters exist for this reason.
      Soil tests. Your state university runs a lab: UMass (Amherst), Cornell, UConn, UNH, URI, UMaine, Penn State, Rutgers. Fees run about $15 to $30, results come with recommendations, and pH is the number that matters most. Rhododendrons, blueberries, and blue hydrangeas want it under 5.5; most everything else is happy between 6 and 7.

      Design, in five rules

      Layers. A bed reads well when it has a back (shrubs or a small tree), a middle (2 to 4 foot perennials and small shrubs), and a front (under 18 inches, including groundcover). The finder builds in these layers because the eye expects them.

      Repetition. Repeat the same plant in groups of three, five, or seven, and repeat one or two plants along the whole length of a bed. Twelve different plants in a row reads as a nursery; four plants, repeated, reads as a garden.

      Succession. Plan for something in flower every month from March to October: bulbs and witch hazel early, the shrub flush in May, perennials in June and July, and the late shift (asters, goldenrod, sedum, grasses) that most yards forget. The library's bloom filter finds the gaps.

      Structure. Roughly a third of the plants that show from the street should be evergreen or have winter shape (bark, seed heads, berries), or the yard disappears for five months.

      Right plant, right place. The cheapest way to have a low-maintenance garden is to plant things that want the conditions you have, rather than amending the conditions to suit things you saw in a catalog.
